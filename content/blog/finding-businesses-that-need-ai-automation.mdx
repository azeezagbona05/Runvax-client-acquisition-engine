To find businesses that need AI automation, look for a visible operational gap rather than an industry or a job title: enquiries that go unanswered, a task someone does by hand every day that a system could do, or no cover for messages and calls outside business hours. A gap you can see and name is a gap you can sell against. An industry label — "restaurants", "clinics" — is just a filter, and on its own it puts every business in the category on the same list whether they need you or not.

## The Gaps Worth Targeting

| Gap | How you spot it | What you sell |
|---|---|---|
| Missed enquiries | Reviews mention slow replies; no live chat; contact form to a shared inbox | AI enquiry agent + capture |
| No after-hours cover | Small team, owner-operated, long stated response times | 24/7 enquiry and booking handling |
| Manual booking | Phone-only booking; a person managing a paper or spreadsheet calendar | Automated booking workflow |
| Repetitive data entry | Owner mentions "we re-key everything" on a call | Workflow automation between their tools |
| Slow follow-up | Quotes and proposals sent once, never chased | Automated follow-up sequence |

The first two are visible from the public record before you speak to anyone. The rest surface on a discovery call, which is why the first message should open on a visible gap and use the call to find the others.

## Disqualify Before You Qualify

The mistake that wastes the most time is running the full check on every business in the list. Verification is cheap at ten seconds and expensive at five minutes, so run it in order of how fast it can rule a business out:

1. **Is it a chain or a franchise?** (Two seconds.) A local branch of a national brand cannot buy a website or an enquiry system — head office decides, and the manager you reach has no budget and no authority. Category searches surface these constantly and they are the single most common dead end.
2. **Is the business actually trading?** (Ten seconds.) Recent reviews, operational status, a phone number. A dormant listing looks like a perfect opportunity precisely because every gap is present. The [seven signals post](/blog/signals-a-local-business-needs-an-ai-agent) covers why this check has to come before opportunity scoring, not after.
3. **Is the transaction big enough?** (Ten seconds, from trade knowledge.) A booking worth £2,000 justifies a build. A £15 one does not, no matter how bad their enquiry handling is.
4. **Do they already have someone?** (Thirty seconds.) A recently rebuilt site, a live chat widget already installed, an agency credit in the footer. Not always fatal — but you are now displacing an incumbent, which is a different and slower sale.
5. **Only now**, run the full gap check.

Most lists lose 50–70% of their rows in steps one to four. That is the point: the five minutes you save on each of those is what makes checking the survivors properly affordable.

## Category Sets the Ceiling; Signals Place the Business

An industry tells you how much automation could plausibly help — a busy clinic has more to gain than a sole-trader accountant. But two clinics in the same city are not equal prospects. One has 600 reviews, a booking page, and a full front desk. The other has 80 reviews, phone-only booking, and a one-line review saying "rang three times, no answer".

Good prospecting uses the category to set the range and business-specific signals to place each business inside it. Without the second step, your daily list is just an industry directory.

This also changes how you open. When your case rests on something you observed — a review, a missing booking flow — you can quote it. When it rests only on the category, you are inferring, and the message has to be framed as a question rather than a diagnosis. Knowing which of the two you are holding is the difference between a confident opener and an embarrassing one.

## Matching the Gap to What You Actually Build

A gap is only useful if it maps to something you can deliver. In practice local-business work resolves into a small number of builds:

| Build | The gap it answers |
|---|---|
| Support / enquiry handling | Questions arriving faster than anyone answers them |
| Qualification | Plenty of enquiries, most of them not worth the team's time |
| Booking | Appointments taken by phone, or a calendar maintained by hand |
| Sales follow-up | Quotes sent once and never chased |
| Operations | The same information re-typed between two systems |
| Website build | No site, or a site missing what customers keep asking for |

That last row is the fork people miss. A customer complaining they could not find prices online is describing a missing page, not a missing agent. Pitch an enquiry system there and you are solving a problem the business does not have.

## One Angle Per Message

Once you have the gap, the temptation is to list every improvement you spotted. Resist it. A first message should argue from exactly one lens — the missed enquiry, the staff workload, the speed of response, or the customer's experience — and never two.

Two reasons. A message making one specific claim is answerable; a message making four reads as a generic audit and invites no reply. And if the owner disagrees with one of your four points, the whole message is now wrong rather than partly right.

Pick the angle the strongest evidence supports, lead with it, and keep the rest for the call.

## Verifying the Gap Before You Pitch

- **Check the website** for a booking flow, live chat, and where the contact form goes.
- **Read the recent reviews** — the last 10, not the all-time average — for response-time complaints.
- **Note the team size** from photos, "meet the team" pages, and review mentions.
- **Estimate the transaction value.** A £2,000 booking justifies the build; a £15 one does not.
