Web developers find clients in 2026 the same way they always have, plus tooling that removes the grind: proactive outbound to businesses with a visible technical gap (no website, a slow or broken site, a dead booking flow, a page builder hitting its limits), combined with a tracked follow-up sequence of 4 to 5 touches over about three weeks. Waiting for referrals and marketplace bids still works for some, but it is not a pipeline you control.

## Developer Gaps Pay More Than Design Gaps

A designer sells "your site looks dated". A developer can sell the things that have a number attached:

| Gap | The business cost | The developer pitch | How you find it |
|---|---|---|---|
| No website | Invisible in search; every competitor with a site wins the click | Build and launch | Detected from the public business listing |
| Slow site | 53% of mobile visitors leave after 3 seconds; conversions drop up to 20% per extra second | Rebuild on a fast stack | Google PageSpeed mobile score; Runvax flags anything under 50 out of 100 once scanned |
| No booking or enquiry capture | Every form into a shared inbox is a lost lead | Booking system plus CRM capture | Click through the site yourself; reviews mentioning hard booking are a hint |
| Outgrown a page builder | Wix or Squarespace limits on speed, integrations, custom logic | Migration to a real stack | Inspect the site; the tool does not detect this |
| No after-hours enquiry cover | Weekend messages sit until Monday; the lead calls a competitor | AI enquiry and booking layer | Inferred from the trade, not observed per business |

The last row is the upsell that turns a one-off build into a retainer, and it is why [the client acquisition page for AI agent builders](/for-ai-agent-builders) is worth reading even if you think of yourself as "just" a developer.

Notice the right-hand column. Some gaps can be found automatically and some need you to look. Being clear with yourself about which is which keeps your first line honest, and honest first lines get replies.

## Step 1: Build the List by Gap

Pick one or two gaps and one industry, then one city. "Dental clinics in Leeds with no website" is a list. "Small businesses that need a website" is not.

Check that each business is real before you spend time on it. A business with no website and no recent activity is often closed or dormant, which makes it look like a perfect target and a worthless one. Quick checks: a handful of Google reviews from the last few months, a working phone number, opening hours listed. ## Step 2: Verify the Problem Yourself

Before you write anything, confirm the gap in two minutes:

1. **Speed.** Run the site through Google PageSpeed Insights on mobile. Note the score and the single biggest thing slowing it down.
2. **Function.** On your phone, try to book, call and use the contact form. Note anything that fails.
3. **Findability.** Search the business name plus the city. Note what appears and what does not.

Write down only what you saw. Never quote a load time you did not measure; a prospect who tests it and gets a different number will not reply to touch two.

## Step 3: The Outbound That Works

1. **Name the specific problem in line one.** "Your site scores 31 out of 100 on Google PageSpeed on mobile, and your contact form did not send when I tried it" beats any portfolio link for a cold open. Use your real numbers.
2. **Lead with the outcome and a bounded first step.** "I can have a fast, bookable version live in two weeks" rather than "I do full-stack development".
3. **Keep it short.** Three or four sentences, one ask, one link at most.
4. **Follow up 4 to 5 times, varying the angle.** Touch two is the before/after speed result; touch three is a competitor comparison; touch four is a short mockup or a screen recording of their own site; touch five is a polite close. New value each time, never "just checking in". See [how many follow-ups before giving up](/blog/how-many-follow-ups-before-giving-up).
5. **Track everything.** Most lost deals die between a warm reply and the next message.

For a business with no website at all, the case is simpler and stronger. The absence is directly observable and needs no argument, and in Runvax's scoring it carries the highest ceiling of any opportunity type (96 out of 100), against 70 for a slow existing site. That is a useful signal about the conversation itself: a slow-site pitch has to win an argument first, so expect a lower reply rate and plan more touches for it. ## Handling the Common Replies

- **"We already have a website."** Reply with the measured score and one specific fix, not a sales pitch. The aim is a ten-minute call, not a sale.
- **"How much?"** Give a range and what drives it, then ask one question about their goal. A number with no context invites a comparison with the cheapest quote they can find.
- **"My nephew is doing it."** Acknowledge it, leave the measured problem with them, and set a reminder for a month out. Cheap builds often stall.
- **"Not now."** Ask when to follow up and put it in your pipeline with a date.

## Portfolio vs Pipeline

A focused portfolio helps, but it is not a prerequisite for outbound. For a cold first touch, a message that names a real, specific problem on the prospect's current site outperforms a link to your best work. Build the portfolio from the first few outbound wins, and keep one before-and-after example ready for touch three.

## Moving From One-Off Builds to Retainers

The first project is rarely the most valuable one. After delivery, the second conversation is about what happens to enquiries now that the site works: after-hours messages, booking, follow-up. That is the AI enquiry layer, and it is a monthly retainer, not a one-off fee. Pitch it as the same kind of outcome you just delivered, more customers reaching the business, rather than as new technology.
